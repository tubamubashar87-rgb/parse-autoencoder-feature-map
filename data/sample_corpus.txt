The transformer architecture processes sequences of tokens through stacked layers of self-attention and feed-forward sublayers, each of which reads from and writes to a shared residual stream.
Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse engineer the algorithms implemented inside a trained neural network by studying its weights and activations directly, rather than treating the model as a black box.
Sparse autoencoders decompose a dense activation vector into a much larger set of sparsely active features, under the hypothesis that neural networks represent more concepts than they have neurons, a phenomenon known as superposition.
A residual stream activation at a given layer is the sum of every earlier layer's output that has been written into it, so it can be thought of as a shared communication channel that every attention head and MLP reads from and writes to.
Dictionary learning methods such as sparse coding were originally developed to explain the receptive fields of neurons in the visual cortex using an overcomplete basis of edge-like filters.
Polysemantic neurons respond to multiple unrelated concepts, which makes individual neurons a poor unit of analysis; a linear combination of neurons is often a cleaner unit, and sparse autoencoders attempt to discover exactly those combinations.
Training a sparse autoencoder involves minimizing a reconstruction loss between the original activation and its reconstruction, plus an L1 penalty on the feature activations that encourages most features to be zero for any given input.
The feature density of a learned dictionary element is the fraction of tokens on which it fires; extremely low density features are often specific and interpretable, while dead features never fire at all and contribute nothing.
Resampling dead neurons during training, using an appropriate learning rate schedule, and tying or untying encoder and decoder weights are all practical considerations that affect how interpretable the resulting dictionary is.
Interpretability researchers often evaluate a sparse autoencoder using the fraction of variance explained by the reconstruction, the average number of features active per token, and manual inspection of the tokens that maximally activate individual features.
Circuit analysis builds on top of a feature dictionary by tracing how individual features causally influence downstream computation and the model's final output logits.
The GPT-2 small model has twelve transformer blocks, a residual stream width of seven hundred and sixty eight dimensions, and twelve attention heads per block.
Attention heads move information between token positions by computing a weighted sum over value vectors, where the weights are determined by the similarity between query and key vectors.
Multilayer perceptron sublayers apply the same nonlinear transformation independently to every token position and are believed to store a large fraction of a model's factual and associative knowledge.
Activation patching is a causal intervention technique where an activation from one forward pass is substituted into another forward pass to measure its effect on the output, helping to localize which components matter for a given behavior.
